GREENSPACE · PERSONAL FIELD JOURNAL

Growing
beyond
ordinary.

A long-term record of growing, experimenting, propagating and grafting plants from around the world under the conditions of my own garden in Bangladesh.

GROW → EXPERIMENT → PROPAGATE → GRAFT → ADAPT → LEARN

It started with a seed.

What began as simple attempts to grow fruit from seeds gradually became years of experiments in germination, propagation, grafting and climate adaptation.

GreenSpace is a record of those experiments — including the successes, failures, unexpected results and lessons learned along the way.

PART 01

From Seed

Before grafting and propagation came the simplest question: can I grow this?

FIELD NOTE · STRAWBERRY

A small experiment with a surprisingly large lesson.

I collected some strawberry seeds directly from fruit and compared them with seeds bought from shops. The seeds from fruit performed much better; very few of the store-bought seeds sprouted.

In winter 2024, I dried both types, then tested germination using a wet paper towel in a container. I also compared refrigeration with room-temperature conditions.

The refrigerated seeds germinated more successfully, although room-temperature seeds also worked. After germination, I planted the seedlings in small pots and discovered another challenge: young strawberry plants were extremely sensitive during replanting.

I also noticed differences in flowering and fruiting depending on temperature under my conditions.

RESULT · Successful germination, growth, flowering and fruiting.

FIELD NOTE · APPLE

A difficult start.

Apple seeds were collected from fruit and tested using the same basic germination approach.

Under my conditions, refrigeration was essential for germination. Planting also required care and survival rates were relatively low.

Interestingly, grafting later proved much more successful than simply growing the apple plants from seed.

RESULT · Difficult from seed, much more promising through grafting.

FIELD NOTE · CHERRY

Similar method, better survival.

Cherry followed a similar germination process to apple, but with better germination and survival. The experiment dates back to around 2020.

FIELD NOTE · TAMARIND

One of the easiest.

Tamarind worked with several methods. Both refrigerated and non-refrigerated seeds germinated, but the paper-towel method without refrigeration performed best.

Direct soil planting also worked, and survival was excellent.

DRAGON FRUIT

Patience was the real requirement.

Germination took roughly two months, followed by another two months of extremely slow early growth. Both water-bowl and paper-towel methods worked, although paper towel performed better.

DATE

Slow, but incredibly hardy.

Dates behaved similarly to dragon fruit, but germinated faster while growing slowly at first. The plants proved extremely hardy, with 100% survival in my experience.

FIELD NOTE · CITRUS

Different fruits, almost identical experience.

Lime, lemon, tangerine and orange behaved very similarly in my experiments.

The paper-towel method produced excellent germination and sprouting, and the young plants were relatively easy to keep alive.

RESULT · Excellent germination and easy establishment.

What came next was different.

Healthy plants became the starting point for something more experimental.

LESSON

Rooting successfully ≠ establishing a successful new tree.

Some young trees rooted beautifully but later died because maintaining them after separation required much more care and watering.

METHOD 02

One tree. Multiple possibilities.

Conventional grafting became another way to combine varieties and experiment with getting seed-grown plants to fruit sooner.

50–70% Approximate grafting success across my experiments.

Successful grafts generally established in around two weeks. When a graft failed, I usually tried again rather than abandoning the experiment.

ONGOING PROJECT · STARTED 2019

Aiming for ten varieties on one tree.

Mango became one of my longest-running grafting experiments. The goal is to combine ten different mango varieties onto a single tree.

There are currently four varieties on it, and I am still working toward the larger goal.

Mango grafting has also produced plenty of failures, which means the project is constantly being monitored, retried and adjusted.

STATUS · Ongoing

PART 03

Exotics

Can foreign plants learn to live here?

OBSERVATION · NATURAL ADAPTERS

Some plants simply fit in.

I grew a wide range of plants originating from different parts of the world and found that many survived and thrived with relatively ordinary care.

Plants that performed well included rosemary, Chinese orange, dragon fruit, cherry, cashew, star gooseberry, Indian gooseberry, sweet olive, mulberry, soursop, miracle berry, sapodilla, almond and others.

Foreign plants generally received additional attention through compost, pest protection, watering and observation.

FIELD NOTE · OBSERVATION

Survival was only the first test.

Keeping a foreign plant alive was one thing. Watching how it responded to local heat, rainfall, pests, soil and seasonal changes was a completely different part of the experiment.

The plants that struggled taught me just as much as the ones that grew easily.

CASE STUDY · CLIMATE ADAPTATION

Teaching cool-climate plants to handle heat.

Apple, peach, strawberry and blackberry were much more difficult under the tropical conditions of Bangladesh.

01

Start cool

Lower temperature, lower sunlight and controlled watering.

02

Increase gradually

Roughly once a month, sunlight, temperature and water were increased.

03

Normal conditions

The plants were gradually exposed to ordinary local growing conditions.

04

Thriving

The surviving plants now grow under normal conditions and are fruiting.

FIELD NOTE · BACKUP PLANTS

Experimenting without losing everything.

Some foreign plants were fragile enough that experimenting directly with them felt risky. Propagation gave me a way to keep younger plants available as backups.

Interestingly, plants raised from the beginning under local conditions often required less special treatment later.

ORIGINAL PLANT → PROPAGATION → YOUNG PLANTS → LOCAL CONDITIONS

FAILED EXPERIMENT · CACAO

Not everything survives.

I managed to keep a cacao bean tree alive for a while, but eventually lost it. It remains one of the experiments that didn't make it to the finish line.

RESULT · Failed, but documented.

WHY GROW THEM?

Awareness · Agriculture · Opportunity

Part of the motivation behind growing foreign fruits is simple curiosity. But there is also a bigger idea: helping people recognize unfamiliar fruits and plants, understand that they can potentially be grown locally, and see the agricultural and financial possibilities they may represent.
